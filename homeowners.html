First, the honest part

Most of the refinance offers in your mailbox aren't for your benefit.

If you own a home, you get mail and calls about refinancing constantly. Almost none of it is based on your actual loan, your actual rate, or how long you plan to stay.

A refinance is worth doing when the math works for you — when the savings clear the costs inside a timeframe that matches your plans, or when restructuring solves a real problem like high-interest debt or a payment that's squeezing you.

Plenty of the time, the right answer is: don't. I'll tell you that, and I'll show you the numbers behind it. A refinance you shouldn't have done is expensive in a way that doesn't show up for years.

Refinancing

Four reasons to refinance. Only do it if one is genuinely yours.

01

Lower the payment

The classic reason — when the numbers support it.

If rates have moved since you closed, or your credit and equity have improved, a lower rate can meaningfully cut what you send out every month. The test isn't the rate itself, it's the break-even: how long before the savings exceed the cost of doing it, and are you staying that long?

02

Shorten the term

Own it outright sooner, pay far less in total interest.

Moving from a 30-year to a 15- or 20-year loan usually raises the monthly payment but can save a striking amount over the life of the loan. This one matters most if retirement is in view and you'd rather not carry a mortgage into it.

03

Use your equity

Cash-out, when it's solving a real problem.

Your equity can fund a renovation, consolidate high-interest debt, or cover something life put in front of you. It's a powerful tool and a permanent one — you're moving debt onto your house. We'll look hard at whether it genuinely improves your position or just moves the problem somewhere quieter.

04

Change the loan itself

Drop mortgage insurance, leave an adjustable rate, restructure.

Sometimes the rate isn't the issue — the structure is. Enough equity may let you shed mortgage insurance. An adjustable-rate loan may be approaching an adjustment you'd rather not meet. These refinances quietly save people a lot and rarely get talked about.

Downsizing

Less house. More life. A sequence that has to be handled carefully.

Downsizing sounds simple until you're doing it: sell first or buy first, where you live in between, what the equity actually nets you, and how a new payment fits a fixed or changing income. It deserves patience and planning, not a rushed pre-approval.

The equity picture

What your current home is likely to net after costs, and what that leaves for the next purchase — before you list anything.

The sequence

Buy first, sell first, or overlap. Each has a real cost and a real risk. We'll pick the one that fits your tolerance, not the fastest one.

The new payment

What the smaller home actually costs monthly, including taxes and insurance — which don't always shrink the way people expect.

The timeline

Two transactions coordinated so you're never homeless and never carrying two payments longer than you planned.
